> The best way to understand people is often not to ask them what they think, but to observe what they reveal when they are not consciously trying to answer a question.

Traditional surveys appear to offer a direct path to understanding public opinion: ask enough people the same question, aggregate the answers, and infer what society thinks. But the act of asking can change the answer. A small change in wording can produce dramatically different responses. Asking whether someone would personally take an extreme action feels different from asking whether another person “deserves” that action, even when the underlying issue is identical. The respondent is no longer answering only the substantive question. They are also responding to the frame in which the question was presented.

There is another problem. People do not always report what they actually believe. Even in anonymous surveys, we know that some answers appear more respectable, socially acceptable, or morally defensible than others. When someone knows they are being evaluated, they may unconsciously answer as the person they would like to be seen as rather than as the person they actually are. This is why behavioral data can sometimes reveal something surveys miss. When people search, post, comment, share, or react online, they are usually not thinking, “I am currently participating in a research study.” Their behavior is generated for another purpose. That makes it possible to observe preferences in a less artificial environment.

The distinction is subtle but important: **Surveys measure what people say when asked. Behavioral data measures what people do when they are not being asked.** Neither is automatically more truthful. A social-media post can also be performative. Online populations are not representative of the entire population. Algorithms determine which content becomes visible, and the loudest people may produce far more data than everyone else. But large-scale behavioral data gives us something traditional surveys cannot easily provide: enormous volume, continuous observation, and the ability to detect patterns across countries, products, and events almost in real time.

Machine learning makes this possible by turning unstructured language into measurable signals. Posts can be classified into emotions such as happiness, anger, anxiety, or sadness by learning patterns in words, expressions, punctuation, syntax, and other features. The technical method matters, but the deeper idea matters more to me. Data does not become useful simply because there is more of it. What matters is how the data was generated. A million answers to a badly framed question can still produce a misleading conclusion. At the same time, millions of naturally occurring observations may reveal patterns that no respondent would consciously report.

This is useful far beyond politics. A company evaluating a new product feature may learn more from how customers actually talk about and use the product than from asking whether they “like” it. An organization trying to understand employee sentiment may learn something different from communication patterns than from an annual satisfaction survey. The broader lesson is that measurement itself changes what we observe. Whenever I see a statistic claiming to represent people's preferences, I should not immediately ask whether the sample is large.

I should first ask: What situation produced this answer? Were people describing what they truly felt? Were they responding to the way the question was framed? Were they trying to present themselves in a particular way? Or were their preferences revealed indirectly through behavior? Sometimes the most valuable data is not the answer people deliberately give us. It is the trace they leave behind when nobody asks them a question.
