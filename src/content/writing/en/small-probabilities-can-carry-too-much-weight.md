> We do not always treat probability according to its numerical size. A vivid possibility can receive far more weight in our decisions than its actual likelihood deserves.

Buying lottery tickets and buying insurance seem like opposite behaviors.

A lottery ticket pays off only if an extremely unlikely good event occurs. Insurance protects us from an extremely unlikely bad event. If people simply disliked risk, buying lottery tickets would be difficult to explain. If they simply liked risk, buying insurance would be equally difficult to explain.

Both behaviors make more sense once we recognize that people tend to overweight small, vivid probabilities.

A one-in-a-million chance does not always feel one million times less important than certainty. When the outcome is sufficiently striking — becoming suddenly wealthy, losing a house, suffering a major accident — the possibility itself can occupy a disproportionate amount of mental space.

This is different from simply misestimating probability.

Someone may know perfectly well that the probability of winning a lottery is tiny and still buy a ticket. The error is not necessarily believing that the probability is higher than it really is. The person may simply give that tiny probability too much weight when choosing.

That distinction matters.

It means that correcting people's factual estimates does not automatically correct their decisions.

The same preference appears in financial markets.

Some stocks resemble lotteries. They are cheap, volatile, close to bankruptcy, newly listed, or surrounded by a story that creates enormous upside imagination. Most of these outcomes will never materialize, but there is always a visible possibility that one company becomes the next extraordinary winner.

Investors are therefore willing to pay for the dream.

The consequence is similar to an actual lottery. When many people are willing to overpay for a small probability of an extreme gain, the expected return on the asset can become unattractive. The most exciting stocks can therefore produce lower average returns precisely because investors enjoy owning them.

This gives an interesting interpretation of IPOs and distressed stocks.

An IPO gives investors a fresh narrative with few historical constraints. A stock that has fallen from $20 to $1 creates an equally powerful story: perhaps it will return to $20. Neither possibility is impossible. The mistake is allowing the dramatic upside scenario to dominate the much larger set of ordinary outcomes.

In that sense, investing can quietly turn into entertainment.

The lecture also connects this preference with loss aversion. Our demand for lottery-like outcomes is not constant. After losses, the desire for a dramatic recovery can become stronger. Once I am below my reference point, a small chance of getting back to even can suddenly become much more attractive.

This is why gambling often becomes more dangerous after losing.

The goal changes from making a good decision to recovering the previous position.

Markets offer the opposite opportunity as well: selling insurance.

Merger arbitrage is one example. After an acquisition is announced, the target company's stock often trades slightly below the announced purchase price because there remains a small probability that the deal fails. Buying the target at that discount is economically similar to selling insurance against the failure of the deal.

Most of the time, the investor earns a small return.

Occasionally, the loss can be severe.

That distinction is crucial. Selling insurance is profitable only if the small gains are sufficient compensation for the rare disaster and if the investor can survive when that disaster occurs. One apparently attractive expected return means little if a single event can destroy the portfolio.

The broader lesson is not that small probabilities should be ignored.

They should be treated as small probabilities.

Before acting on a dramatic possibility, I should separate two questions:

How likely is this outcome?

And how much emotional weight am I giving it simply because it is vivid?

Sometimes the most expensive thing in a market is not the asset itself.

It is the story attached to a tiny chance that everything changes.
