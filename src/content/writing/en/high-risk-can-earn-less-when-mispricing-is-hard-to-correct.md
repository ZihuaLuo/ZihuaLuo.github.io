> Risk does not determine return by itself. The relationship changes when investor preferences create mispricing and market frictions prevent that mispricing from being corrected.

One of the most basic ideas in finance is that higher return should require higher risk. That principle is intuitive. If two investments offer the same expected return, investors should prefer the safer one. The risky asset therefore needs to offer additional compensation before anyone is willing to hold it. Yet one of the most persistent findings in financial markets points in the opposite direction: historically, low-volatility stocks have often produced higher returns than high-volatility stocks. This is the low-volatility anomaly. What makes the result interesting is not simply that a profitable strategy exists. The more important question is why such a relationship can survive when it appears to contradict one of finance's most fundamental principles.

The first explanation comes from behavioral preferences. Our willingness to take risk is not constant. Prospect theory suggests that people behave differently depending on whether they feel ahead or behind a reference point. When investors are sitting on gains, they become more interested in protecting those gains. Risk feels less attractive. Under those conditions, risky assets may genuinely need to offer higher expected returns to attract buyers. But after losses, preferences change. Investors may become more willing to gamble in an attempt to recover. High-volatility stocks suddenly become more attractive because they provide a greater possibility of getting back to the reference point.

The same asset characteristic can therefore be priced differently depending on investors' psychological state. This already gives a more useful way to think about investment signals. Instead of asking whether volatility predicts return, I should ask under what conditions volatility predicts return. But the deeper explanation combines behavioral demand with limits to arbitrage. Suppose two stocks are both overpriced, but one is much more volatile. A rational investor would like to short both. The problem is that shorting the high-volatility stock is much more dangerous. Even if the investor is ultimately correct about its fundamental value, the price can move dramatically higher before correcting.

That creates exactly the problem discussed earlier with LTCM: being right eventually does not help if the position cannot survive the path. As a result, arbitrageurs correct the low-volatility stock more aggressively while leaving more of the high-volatility stock's overpricing intact. The high-risk stock remains more overpriced. Its future return becomes lower. Now consider the opposite situation, where both stocks are undervalued. Arbitrageurs can simply buy them. There are fewer institutional barriers to buying than to shorting, so undervaluation is easier to correct.

This creates an asymmetry. High volatility can amplify mispricing in both directions, but overpricing is harder to eliminate because correcting it often requires short selling. The negative relationship between risk and future return among overpriced stocks can therefore be much stronger than the positive relationship among underpriced stocks. Average the two together, and the strange result becomes possible: **higher-risk stocks can earn lower returns.** What I find most useful here is not the low-volatility strategy itself. Once a strategy becomes widely known, capital naturally flows toward it and some of the opportunity should disappear.

The more durable lesson is how the anomaly is explained. Behavior alone is not enough. Market frictions alone are not enough. The interesting result appears when the two interact. Investors create demand that pushes some prices away from fundamentals. Volatility determines how dangerous it is to bet against that mispricing. Short-sale constraints make correcting overvaluation harder than correcting undervaluation. Together, these forces determine where pricing errors can survive. This is a much richer framework than simply saying that markets are irrational. Whenever I encounter an apparent anomaly, I should ask three questions:

What behavioral force could create the mispricing? Which securities should be most affected? And what prevents rational investors from eliminating it? If I cannot answer the third question, the anomaly should probably disappear quickly. If I can, then what looks like a violation of financial theory may actually reveal where theory's simplifying assumptions stop describing the real market.
