> Traits do not have fixed economic value. The same instinct that improves performance in one environment can become a source of error when the environment changes.

This lecture appears to be about hormones, but the more interesting idea is not whether testosterone makes people more aggressive or whether oxytocin makes people more trusting. It is that human characteristics are rarely simply “good” or “bad.” Their value depends on the situation in which they operate. Testosterone provides a useful example. It is tempting to think of it as a hormone that mechanically produces aggression. A better interpretation is that it can amplify tendencies that are already present. An individual does not suddenly behave without regard to context. Existing competitive, emotional, or risk-taking responses can simply become stronger.

Oxytocin contains a similar contradiction. It can strengthen trust and attachment toward people we already consider part of our group. Yet stronger attachment to insiders does not necessarily mean greater friendliness toward everyone. The same mechanism that increases cooperation within a group can also sharpen the boundary between insiders and outsiders. The same pattern appears in financial markets. Studies of short-term traders suggest that higher testosterone can sometimes accompany better performance. This initially sounds surprising. Finance usually teaches that emotional control is essential and that greater impulsiveness should hurt investment results.

But the trading environment matters. For an intraday trader, hesitation itself can be costly. Decisions must be made quickly, uncertainty cannot be eliminated, and waiting for perfect information may mean losing the opportunity entirely. In such an environment, greater confidence, willingness to take risk, and decisiveness can be useful. Extend the horizon, however, and the relationship can reverse. Longer-term investment gives an investor time to analyze information. Speed becomes less important, while patience and restraint become more valuable. The same tendency toward confidence and action can now produce excessive trading, unnecessary risk taking, preference for lottery-like securities, or an unwillingness to admit mistakes.

A trait that helped under one time horizon becomes harmful under another. This distinction changes how I think about personal strengths more generally. We often describe people using fixed labels: decisive, cautious, confident, aggressive, patient. Then we treat those characteristics as permanent advantages or disadvantages. But there is no universally optimal level of confidence or caution. Too little confidence can prevent action when speed matters. Too much confidence can produce overtrading when patience matters. Strong loyalty can support cooperation inside a team while simultaneously making it harder to evaluate outsiders objectively. Risk tolerance can create entrepreneurship and innovation, but the same appetite for risk can also increase leverage and expose an organization to unnecessary failure.

The mistake is judging a trait without first asking what environment rewards it. This is especially important in finance because success itself can reinforce the wrong behavior. A trader who takes aggressive risks and happens to succeed may interpret the outcome as evidence that greater aggression is always better. But the behavior may only have been well suited to one particular market, strategy, or time horizon. When the environment changes, the strength remains while its usefulness disappears. The broader lesson is therefore not that we should eliminate emotion, hormones, or instinct and become perfectly rational decision-makers. These mechanisms exist because they have often been useful.

The real challenge is alignment. A strength is valuable only when the environment rewards the behavior it produces. Good judgment begins with knowing not only what my tendencies are, but also when those tendencies should be trusted — and when they should be restrained.
