> Following other people is not always irrational. The danger begins when everyone starts treating everyone else’s behavior as information.

Herding is usually described as a failure to think independently. We see other people buying, selling, choosing, or believing something, and we copy them without doing our own analysis.

But the more interesting part of herding is that it can begin completely rationally.

Suppose I am choosing between two restaurants. I have some private information suggesting that restaurant B is slightly better, but I see several people already eating at restaurant A. Their choices contain information. Perhaps they know something I do not.

Following them can therefore be reasonable.

The problem is what happens next.

The second person sees the first person enter A and follows. The third person now sees two people inside A and becomes even more confident. Soon everyone chooses A, even though most people may privately have information favoring B.

At that point, the crowd looks like overwhelming evidence.

But much of that “evidence” is simply repeated observation of the same early decision.

This is an information cascade. A small initial event can determine the behavior of an entire group because later participants rationally infer information from earlier choices.

That makes herding more dangerous than simple imitation.

A crowd can become highly confident without accumulating much independent information.

The same mechanism appears in everyday situations. If someone collapses in public and the first person walking past does nothing, the second observer may reasonably infer that the situation is not serious. The third person sees two people ignoring it and becomes even more confident that intervention is unnecessary.

Eventually, everyone may behave the same way.

Not because everyone is indifferent, but because everyone is learning from everyone else.

Humans also appear to have a deeper tendency to imitate. From an evolutionary perspective, this makes sense. Our ancestors could not independently rediscover every useful behavior. If previous generations prepared food in a certain way, avoided certain plants, or followed particular customs, copying them was often much safer than demanding a complete explanation first.

Imitation allowed knowledge to survive even when people did not understand why the behavior worked.

In a relatively stable environment, that was powerful.

In a rapidly changing environment, the same instinct can create mismatch.

Bad habits can spread just as easily as good ones. Dishonesty inside an organization can become contagious. When people observe colleagues bending rules without consequences, the behavior becomes more normal. Once enough people participate, the social signal changes from “this is wrong” to “this is how things are done here.”

That is why culture can deteriorate surprisingly quickly.

The same logic applies to financial markets.

When more households allocate more of their wealth to equities, that may initially reflect genuinely positive information. But eventually rising prices themselves attract more investors. Those new investors become additional evidence for the next group, which attracts still more capital.

Price increases create participation, and participation validates the price increases.

The process can continue long after the original information has been absorbed.

This helps explain why the most crowded moment can also be the most dangerous moment.

If almost everyone is already optimistic and heavily invested, there may be few remaining buyers to push prices further. Conversely, when very few people want to own an asset, pessimism may already be embedded in the price.

The lesson is not to automatically do the opposite of the crowd.

A crowd often knows something.

The more useful question is whether the crowd contains independent information.

If one hundred people reached the same conclusion after independently examining the evidence, their agreement is powerful.

If ninety-nine people reached the same conclusion because they saw the first person act, the apparent consensus contains far less information than it seems.

So whenever everyone appears to agree, I should ask:

**How many independent judgments am I actually observing?**

Sometimes a crowd represents accumulated wisdom.

Sometimes it is only one opinion echoed a hundred times.
