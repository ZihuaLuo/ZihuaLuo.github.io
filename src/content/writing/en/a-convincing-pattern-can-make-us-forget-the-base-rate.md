> When a story looks representative, we often mistake resemblance for probability and recent performance for persistent ability.

Representativeness bias begins with a simple mistake: something feels likely because it looks like the kind of thing we expect to see.

If a quiet, organized, detail-oriented person sounds like a librarian, our intuition immediately places him in a library. What we forget is the base rate. If there are vastly more farmers than librarians, the person may still be more likely to be a farmer even if his personality sounds less “representative.”

The description captures our attention. The population underneath it disappears.

The same problem appears when we think about randomness. We expect randomness to look random. A sequence such as heads-tails-heads-tails feels more believable than six heads in a row, even though any specific six-flip sequence has exactly the same probability.

From this comes the gambler's fallacy. After several identical outcomes, we expect the next result to reverse because the sequence has begun to look “too orderly.” But an independent coin, roulette wheel, or die has no memory of what happened before.

More interestingly, the opposite mistake can appear when outcomes reflect both skill and luck.

Suppose a fund manager performs exceptionally well for several years. It is psychologically uncomfortable to believe that someone could simply have been lucky repeatedly. A long sequence of good luck does not look representative of randomness. So we naturally assign more of the performance to skill.

Then we extrapolate.

The manager did well before, so we expect the manager to do well again. A stock has risen, so we expect it to continue rising. A company has produced extraordinary earnings growth, so we assume that growth will persist.

The mistake is not that skill or persistence never exists. The mistake is underestimating how much of an extreme observation can be produced by luck.

This distinction is especially important in investing because prices already contain expectations.

A company that everyone believes will grow rapidly may genuinely remain a better company than its competitors. But if investors have extrapolated its recent success too aggressively, the stock can still become a poor investment. Future earnings do not have to become bad. They only have to be less extraordinary than the market expected.

The reverse is also true. A company that has disappointed investors for years does not need to become exceptional to generate a positive surprise. It only needs to perform less badly than expected.

That is why a good company and a good stock are not always the same thing.

Representativeness bias also explains why chasing past returns is so tempting. Recent performance gives us a simple narrative: winners are talented, successful industries will remain successful, and rising markets are safer than falling ones. But the easier the story is to tell, the easier it becomes to ignore valuation, base rates, and regression toward more normal outcomes.

The broader lesson is that patterns deserve more skepticism precisely when they look most convincing.

Whenever recent evidence seems to tell an obvious story, I should ask two questions before extrapolating it forward:

How common was this outcome before I saw the story?

And how much of what I am calling skill could still be luck?

Sometimes the most dangerous forecast is the one that feels the most natural.
