> Personalized medicine is powerful not because it creates more treatments, but because it helps us stop treating different causes as if they were the same problem.

Many medical decisions begin with symptoms. A patient has a sore throat, fever, or cough. The doctor observes the symptoms, looks at a few indirect indicators, and chooses a treatment that works reasonably well for the average patient. For many common illnesses, this approach is good enough. But “good enough” can hide an important problem: the same symptom can be produced by very different causes. A sore throat may come from a bacterial infection, a viral infection, or several pathogens acting together. Two patients can therefore walk into a hospital with almost identical symptoms while requiring completely different treatments.

This is where traditional diagnosis becomes difficult. A blood test may show elevated white blood cells, which is correlated with bacterial infection. But correlation is not identification. A high white-blood-cell count can have other causes, while a localized bacterial infection may not always produce the expected signal. Even when bacteria are involved, the next question remains: which bacteria? Giving a broad-spectrum antibiotic without knowing the exact pathogen is therefore partly a probabilistic decision. It may work. It may be unnecessary. It may also contribute to antibiotic resistance while doing little for the actual illness.

Genomic sequencing changes the structure of the problem. Instead of inferring the cause indirectly from symptoms, researchers can take a sample from the infected area and sequence the genetic material inside it. Most of the DNA will belong to the patient, but some will come from bacteria, viruses, or other organisms. Those genetic signatures can reveal what is actually present. The diagnosis moves from: “What usually causes symptoms like these?” to: “What biological agents are actually present in this patient?”

That is a much more precise question. What I find most interesting is that this represents a broader shift in medicine from classification by appearance to classification by mechanism. Historically, diseases have often been grouped because patients look similar: the same symptoms, the same organ affected, or the same broad clinical pattern. But as biological measurement becomes more precise, some diseases that appear identical may turn out to be fundamentally different problems. And once the categories change, treatment changes with them.

The major obstacle is no longer purely scientific. It is economic. A technology that can identify pathogens accurately but costs $1,000 per test may be useful in research yet impractical for an ordinary sore throat. If the same test eventually costs $100, however, the entire calculation changes. This is a pattern that appears repeatedly in technology. A capability can exist long before it becomes transformative. Transformation happens when the cost falls far enough that the capability can move from exceptional cases into everyday use.

That is what makes the falling cost of genomic analysis so important. The breakthrough is not merely that we can read biological information. It is that reading it may eventually become cheap enough to influence routine decisions. The broader lesson extends beyond medicine. Whenever different underlying causes produce similar visible outcomes, treating the category as homogeneous creates mistakes. **Better decisions begin by moving one level deeper.** **Do not ask only what the problem looks like.** **Ask what is actually producing it.**
