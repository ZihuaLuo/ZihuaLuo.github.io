> If an organization rewards the wrong outcome, no amount of speeches about the right behavior will compensate for the incentive system.

One of the most useful ideas from optimization theory is surprisingly simple: define an objective, repeatedly move in the direction that improves it, and over time the system will converge toward what that objective rewards. Organizations behave in much the same way. Employees constantly observe what actually produces promotions, bonuses, recognition, and influence. Once they understand the rules, they naturally adjust their behavior to maximize those outcomes. This means that a company's real values are not necessarily the values written on its website.

They are embedded in its reward function. Suppose a company repeatedly tells employees that innovation matters, but evaluates every team almost entirely on next quarter's revenue. Employees will eventually prioritize projects that can produce immediate sales. Management may continue talking about long-term innovation. The incentive system is teaching something else. The same pattern appears in smaller decisions. IBM historically linked patents to employee performance and became an extraordinary producer of patents. When Google reduced its incentives for filing patents, management believed employees would continue doing so because they understood the strategic importance.

They did not. Patent applications declined. When the incentive became meaningful again, applications increased rapidly. Nothing fundamental about the employees had changed. The reward function had changed. This changes how I think about management. When people repeatedly behave in a way that leadership dislikes, the first response is often education: hold another meeting, write another memo, remind everyone of the company's mission. But persistent behavior is usually worth examining from the opposite direction. What is the system currently rewarding?

If engineers are punished more severely for failed experiments than they are rewarded for successful innovation, they will become conservative. If managers are evaluated only on the performance of their own departments, they may resist helping another department even when collaboration would benefit the entire company. If employees are rewarded for the number of tasks completed, they will naturally prefer many easy tasks over one difficult, high-impact problem. In each case, employees may be behaving perfectly rationally. The problem is not motivation.

It is objective design. This is why setting incentives is one of the highest-leverage decisions a founder or manager makes. Once the objective is established, thousands of small decisions begin moving in that direction without requiring direct supervision. But this power also creates danger. A measurable target is rarely identical to the outcome we truly care about. Reward patents too aggressively and people may file low-quality patents. Reward sales alone and employees may sacrifice customer relationships to close short-term deals. Reward speed alone and quality may deteriorate.

**People do not optimize the intention behind a metric.** **They optimize the metric itself.** The challenge is therefore not simply to create stronger incentives. It is to choose an objective whose maximization produces the organization we actually want. The broader lesson applies to personal life as well. My daily behavior is also responding to an implicit reward function. If I reward myself psychologically for being busy, I will create more work. If I value visible short-term results above learning, I will avoid difficult projects whose payoff is uncertain.

So whenever behavior repeatedly contradicts intention, it may be worth asking a different question. Not: Why am I not doing what I say matters? But: What am I actually rewarding myself for? Systems eventually become very good at producing exactly what they are designed to reward. That is why choosing the objective may matter more than optimizing it.
