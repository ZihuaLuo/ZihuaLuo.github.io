> When information feels familiar, clear, or pleasant, we often become less critical of it — and mistake ease of processing for evidence of quality.

Limited attention is not constant. Sometimes we are alert and analytical; other times we process information with much less resistance. This lecture calls the second state cognitive ease. The distinction matters because cognitive ease changes not only what we notice, but also what we believe. When something is repeated, easy to understand, visually attractive, or associated with a positive feeling, the brain treats it as more familiar. Familiarity creates a sense of safety, and safety reduces the need for careful scrutiny. We become more willing to accept information without examining it deeply.

This helps explain findings that initially seem irrational. A more attractive CEO can temporarily increase a company's valuation when investors actually see the CEO's photograph. A company with an easier-to-pronounce name can receive a higher valuation and even experience greater trading liquidity. The underlying cash flows have not necessarily changed. What changed is how easily investors process the information. The important point is not that investors consciously believe that attractive executives or fluent company names create more value. The effect can happen before conscious reasoning begins.

A name that feels familiar is easier to remember. A face that creates a positive impression reduces psychological resistance. Once information is easier to process, confidence in the judgment can increase even when the evidence supporting that judgment has not improved. This creates an important risk: we can confuse processing fluency with fundamental quality. Financial markets make this particularly consequential. Investors cannot analyze every company, every announcement, and every piece of information with equal intensity. Some information therefore receives immediate attention while other information enters prices only gradually.

Post-earnings announcement drift is a useful example. When a company reports unexpectedly good news, its price may rise immediately but continue rising afterward. One explanation is that not everyone processes the information at the same time. If attention is the mechanism, then the effect should become stronger when attention is especially scarce. That is exactly what happens. Earnings announcements released when investors are distracted — such as on Fridays or on days when many other companies report simultaneously — can generate stronger subsequent price drift. The information itself has not become more valuable. Fewer people simply processed it when it first arrived.

This changes how I think about market efficiency. An investment opportunity does not always exist because information is secret. Sometimes the information is completely public, but the market has not allocated enough attention to understand it. The same principle applies outside investing. A familiar argument can feel more convincing simply because I have heard it repeatedly. A polished presentation can make an idea seem stronger than its underlying logic. A person who is easy to listen to can appear more credible than someone communicating a better argument less fluently.

Cognitive ease is useful because constantly questioning everything would be exhausting. But the efficiency comes with a cost. When a conclusion feels immediately comfortable, that may be exactly when I should ask one additional question: **Do I believe this because the evidence is strong, or because believing it feels easy?**
