> An anchor does not need to be informative to influence us. Once a reference point enters our thinking, we often adjust away from it far less than the evidence requires.

Anchoring is usually explained through discounts. A jacket originally priced at $999 suddenly looks attractive at $666 because the original price gives us a reference point. But the more surprising form of anchoring begins when the reference point contains no useful information at all. In classroom experiments, people can first be shown a number derived from the last digits of their phone number and then asked to estimate an unrelated historical date. Those who happen to begin with larger numbers systematically give larger estimates. Similar experiments using Social Security numbers find that people with higher random numbers are willing to pay substantially more for products such as wine, chocolate, or computer accessories.

The number has no information about the product. It still changes the judgment. This suggests that anchoring is not simply a rational response to useful information. Once an initial value becomes mentally available, we often begin reasoning from that point and adjust around it. The problem is that the adjustment is usually insufficient. This becomes much more important when the anchor is not a random number but our own previous belief.

Suppose I believe a company earns $5 per share. New information arrives suggesting that its underlying profitability has improved significantly. I may update my estimate to $5.50, believing that I have incorporated the news. But if the new reality is closer to $7, I have updated in the right direction while still remaining too close to the original belief. Being willing to change my mind is therefore not enough. I also need to change it by the appropriate amount.

This helps explain why prices can continue moving after public information has already been released. Investors may see the same earnings announcement and understand whether it is good or bad, yet remain anchored to previous expectations. A positive surprise moves their forecasts upward, but not far enough. Later information then appears to be another positive surprise, and the price continues drifting. Anchoring also explains why expertise does not automatically eliminate bias. In housing experiments, professional real-estate agents are influenced by asking prices even when they have detailed information about the property. Interestingly, professionals may be less likely than amateurs to admit that the asking price affected their judgment.

That may be the more dangerous form of bias: not being influenced, but being influenced while believing that I am not. The lesson extends beyond numbers. My first interpretation of a person, company, market, or idea can become an anchor as well. Once I have formed a view, new information is rarely processed from a blank slate. It is interpreted relative to what I already believe. That means updating requires more than asking whether the new evidence changes my opinion. A better question is:

**If I had seen all of the evidence today for the first time, without knowing my previous conclusion, would I still arrive at the same answer?** Sometimes the hardest part of changing a belief is not moving in the right direction. It is moving far enough.
