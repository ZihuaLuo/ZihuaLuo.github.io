> Many bad decisions do not come from ignorance. They come from the gap between what we understand and what our brain motivates us to do.

A common assumption in economics is that once people know what is best for them, they will act accordingly. If diversification reduces risk, investors should diversify. If an opportunity has a positive expected payoff, people should pursue it. If a decision is logically correct, understanding the logic should be enough.

But knowing and doing are not the same process.

The distinction becomes clearer when we look at how the brain makes decisions. Daniel Kahneman describes two systems of thinking. System 1 is fast, automatic, and emotional. System 2 is slower, more deliberate, and more analytical. We often think poor decisions happen because System 1 gives us the wrong answer and System 2 fails to correct it. But this lecture adds another layer: even when System 2 reaches the right conclusion, that conclusion may never become action.

Part of the reason is that thought and motivation are driven by different mechanisms.

Dopamine, for example, is often described as the chemical of pleasure. A more useful interpretation is that it helps create anticipation and motivation. Experiments show that animals can still enjoy a reward after dopamine-related pathways are disrupted, but they become much less willing to work for it. They still “like” the reward. They simply stop pursuing it.

This helps explain a puzzle that appears constantly in financial decisions. An investor may fully understand that international diversification is beneficial and still keep most of a portfolio in familiar domestic assets. The obstacle is not necessarily a lack of financial knowledge. Familiar investments simply feel easier to evaluate and easier to act on.

Ambiguity makes this effect stronger.

Consider two boxes. In the first, I know that half the cards are red and half are black. In the second, I know there are red and black cards but not their proportions. Even if the expected probability of drawing red is effectively the same, most people prefer the first box.

The difference is familiarity, not return.

That same preference can quietly shape a portfolio. Domestic companies, industries I work in, or assets I have studied deeply feel more understandable. Because they feel less ambiguous, I may expect more from them and become more willing to invest. Yet familiarity does not make an asset safer. In some cases it creates exactly the opposite problem.

A researcher specializing in cryptocurrency, for example, might naturally feel more comfortable investing heavily in cryptocurrency. But that creates concentration on two levels: career success and personal wealth now depend on the same industry. What feels familiar can actually increase total risk.

This is why education alone cannot eliminate behavioral mistakes. I can understand diversification, expected value, and portfolio theory perfectly and still feel stronger motivation toward what is familiar.

The broader lesson is that better decisions require more than better reasoning.

If I already know the correct answer but repeatedly fail to act on it, learning more may not solve the problem. I may need to change the environment, automate the decision, create rules in advance, or deliberately reduce the influence of emotion and familiarity.

Knowing what I should do is only the first half of rationality.

The second half is building a system that makes me actually do it.
