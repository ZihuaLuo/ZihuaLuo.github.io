> Overconfidence is not always a flaw. It becomes dangerous when confidence changes our behavior but cannot change the outcome we are betting on.

Overconfidence is usually presented as a mistake. We overestimate our intelligence, driving ability, investment skill, and ability to predict the future. But this lecture introduces a more useful distinction: confidence can sometimes improve outcomes precisely because it changes what we do.

Several biases naturally push us toward overconfidence. When something goes well, we tend to credit our ability; when it goes badly, we blame luck. Once we form an opinion, we search more actively for evidence that supports it. We also tend to believe that we have control over outcomes that are partly or completely random.

Over time, these mechanisms make our beliefs about ourselves more optimistic than the evidence alone would justify.

That does not necessarily make them useless.

Imagine pursuing a difficult project with only a small probability of success. If I evaluate that probability with perfect accuracy, I may decide that the effort is not worthwhile. But if confidence makes the reward feel more attainable, it can increase motivation, persistence, and willingness to tolerate setbacks.

Now my belief has changed my behavior, and my behavior can change the probability of success.

Entrepreneurship is an obvious example. Most new ventures fail, yet founders usually do not behave as though failure is the most likely outcome. They work as if their company can succeed. That confidence helps them recruit employees, persuade investors, survive uncertainty, and continue working when the immediate evidence is discouraging.

In situations like this, confidence can become partially self-fulfilling.

Financial markets are different.

If I become extremely confident that a stock will rise and buy more of it, my additional effort does not make the company's future profits higher. I can research harder, trade more frequently, and become increasingly convinced that my thesis is correct, but none of those actions necessarily changes the underlying outcome.

Here, overconfidence loses its productive channel.

Instead, it often produces excessive trading. Investors become too willing to act on weak signals because they believe their information or judgment is superior. More trades create more transaction costs and more opportunities to be wrong. Evidence from both individual investors and professional funds consistently shows that higher turnover tends to be associated with worse performance.

This suggests a simple way to evaluate confidence.

The important question is not whether I am overconfident. It is whether my confidence can influence the outcome itself.

If confidence makes me practice harder, negotiate more persistently, build a better company, prepare more thoroughly, or recover faster from failure, some optimism may be valuable.

If the outcome is largely outside my control — the next market move, a random event, or another person's independent decision — additional confidence may simply encourage me to take more risk without creating any additional advantage.

This also explains why experts can be particularly dangerous to follow. Expertise does not remove overconfidence, and public experts often face incentives to sound more certain than the evidence permits. “I don't know” may be statistically appropriate, but it rarely attracts attention.

The broader lesson is that confidence should not be judged in isolation.

Sometimes believing more strongly makes me act better, and acting better changes reality.

But when reality does not care how strongly I believe, confidence stops being an asset and starts becoming risk.
