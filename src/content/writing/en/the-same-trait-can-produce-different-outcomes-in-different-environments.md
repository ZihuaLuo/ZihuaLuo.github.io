> Behavior is rarely determined by genes or environment alone. What matters is how inherited tendencies interact with the conditions in which they are expressed.

It is tempting to explain human behavior by asking a simple question: is this caused by genes or by the environment? This lecture suggests that the question itself is often wrong.

Genes and environment rarely operate independently.

Even characteristics that seem highly genetic can change with experience. Memory-related regions of the brain, for example, can physically adapt when people repeatedly perform demanding cognitive tasks. At the same time, studies of twins and adopted children suggest that inherited differences still influence behaviors such as trading frequency, preference for familiar assets, and the tendency to chase past performance.

Neither side eliminates the other.

A better way to think about genes is not as a fixed instruction manual, but as a set of possibilities whose expression depends partly on the environment. The same biological predisposition can therefore lead to very different outcomes under different conditions.

This interaction becomes especially important when the environment changes faster than our biology can adapt.

The Dutch Hunger Winter provides a striking example. Children who developed during severe famine were biologically prepared for a world in which food would remain scarce. Their bodies became unusually efficient at conserving energy. That response would have been useful if scarcity had continued.

But the environment changed.

When food later became abundant, the same adaptation increased the risk of obesity and metabolic disease. What was once a useful response became a disadvantage because the environment that produced it no longer matched the environment in which it operated.

The same logic applies to the way we think.

Humans did not evolve by calculating conditional probabilities. Our ancestors learned from frequencies: how many times an animal appeared near a river, how often a particular food caused illness, or how many members of a group survived a certain event. Modern statistics expresses these problems through probabilities and Bayesian updating, but our intuition often still works better when information is presented as counts.

The medical diagnosis example makes this clear. If a rare disease affects only a tiny fraction of the population, even a test that is highly accurate can generate many more false positives than true positives. Yet when people hear that a test is “90% accurate,” their immediate reaction is often to assume that a positive result means roughly a 90% probability of having the disease.

Translate the same problem into natural frequencies — ten sick people out of ten thousand, followed by the number of positive tests in each group — and the correct conclusion suddenly becomes much easier to see.

The mathematics did not change.

The format changed to match the way the human mind naturally processes information.

This gives me a broader way to think about behavioral mistakes. A bias does not necessarily mean that the brain is poorly designed. A behavior that looks irrational today may have been highly effective under the conditions in which it developed.

The real problem is often mismatch.

Instead of asking whether a tendency is rational or irrational in isolation, a better question is: what environment was this tendency designed for, and does that environment still exist?

Many of our weaknesses may simply be strengths operating in the wrong world.
