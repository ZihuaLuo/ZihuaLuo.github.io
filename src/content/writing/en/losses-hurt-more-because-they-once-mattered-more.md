> Loss aversion is not simply an irrational fear of losing. It becomes a problem when an instinct designed to protect survival is attached to reference points that no longer deserve to matter.

Losing $100 usually hurts more than gaining $100 feels good.

This asymmetry is loss aversion. It is often described as another flaw in human decision-making, something rational people should learn to eliminate. But if the same tendency appears so consistently across people — and even in other primates — it is worth asking why it exists in the first place.

The answer becomes clearer when viewed through survival rather than modern finance.

For our ancestors living close to subsistence, gains and losses were not symmetric. Having twice as much food as necessary might improve life slightly. Having half as much could mean starvation. When the downside threatens survival, reacting more strongly to losses is not irrational at all.

Loss aversion could therefore create useful urgency.

If I had failed to find enough food by the end of the week, becoming more motivated and even more willing to take risks might increase my probability of surviving. If someone tried to take resources I already possessed, an unusually strong reaction could also discourage others from doing the same.

The asymmetry made sense because the consequences themselves were asymmetric.

Modern environments preserve the instinct while changing the stakes.

That is where mismatch begins.

One important consequence appears in investing through the disposition effect. Suppose I buy two stocks at $100. One rises to $150 while the other falls to $80. If I need cash, selling the winner feels much easier. The gain becomes real and satisfying. Selling the loser means accepting that the loss has actually happened.

So investors tend to realize gains quickly and keep losses alive.

Psychologically, this makes sense.

Economically, the original purchase price should be almost irrelevant.

Once I already own the stocks, the useful question is not which one has made or lost money relative to $100. The useful question is which asset offers the better expected return from today onward. The market does not care about the number at which I happened to enter.

Yet that historical number becomes a reference point, and the reference point begins controlling the decision.

This is why loss aversion can quietly turn into a sunk-cost problem. Instead of asking what I would choose now, I start designing the future around recovering the past.

The same mechanism explains framing effects.

A 3% “credit-card surcharge” feels worse than losing a 3% “cash discount,” even if the final prices are identical. A nominal salary increase during inflation can feel better than a smaller nominal salary cut during zero inflation, even if purchasing power falls more in the first case.

Nothing economically fundamental has changed.

What changed is whether the outcome is presented as a gain or a loss relative to a reference point.

That distinction matters because reference points are often arbitrary.

A purchase price is history. Last year's salary is history. A previous valuation, target, forecast, or expectation can all become psychological anchors that make losses feel unacceptable even after the underlying situation has changed.

The danger is not feeling loss more strongly. Sometimes that emotional response is useful. The danger is allowing an outdated reference point to determine what counts as a loss.

A better decision rule is therefore to periodically remove the history from the question.

If I did not already own this stock, would I buy it today?

If I had not already spent this money, would I still continue the project?

If this salary, investment, or commitment had never existed before, would I choose it now?

Loss aversion once helped us defend what mattered for survival.

Good judgment today requires deciding whether what I am defending still matters at all.
