> Investor sentiment does not affect every asset equally. Its influence becomes strongest when fundamentals are difficult to anchor and disagreement is hard to correct.

Investor sentiment is often described vaguely as optimism or pessimism in the market. A more useful definition is excess demand or expectation that cannot be fully explained by fundamentals. Seen this way, sentiment is not a separate behavioral bias. It is closer to an aggregation of many of them. Limited attention can make investors focus too heavily on exciting stories. Representativeness can lead them to extrapolate recent success. Overconfidence can make them trust those forecasts too much. Herding can amplify the same belief once other investors begin acting on it.

All of these forces can eventually appear in one place: price. The lecture gives several striking examples of how seemingly unrelated factors can influence markets through sentiment. Weather, sporting results, the day of the week, disrupted sleep, and even the lunar cycle have all been associated with changes in market returns. Whether every individual effect is economically exploitable is less important to me than the underlying idea. Investors do not enter the market as emotionless valuation machines. Mood can affect willingness to take risk, optimism about future outcomes, and ultimately demand for assets.

Cultural preferences can do the same thing. “Sin stocks” provide a particularly interesting example. If some investors avoid tobacco, alcohol, gambling, or similar companies for moral or institutional reasons, demand for those stocks may be lower than their fundamentals alone would imply. If enough investors avoid them, their prices must become cheaper before the remaining investors are willing to hold them. The higher future return, in that case, is compensation not necessarily for conventional financial risk, but for holding something other investors dislike.

This leads to a broader principle: **An asset can become mispriced because of how people feel about owning it, not only because they misunderstand its cash flows.** But sentiment becomes more useful when combined with another question: which assets are easiest for sentiment to move? A hundred-year-old company in a stable industry gives investors a long operating history, established cash flows, and many comparable firms. There is less room for imagination. A young, small, volatile company is different. Much more of its value depends on distant possibilities. When the future is difficult to estimate, optimistic and pessimistic narratives can move valuations much further.

That is why market-wide sentiment may be poor at predicting the entire market while still being useful for predicting differences across stocks. When sentiment is extremely high, speculative companies may become disproportionately expensive relative to mature companies. When sentiment is unusually low, those same companies may become disproportionately cheap. The signal is not simply “sentiment is high, therefore sell everything.” It is more subtle: **Ask which assets sentiment is most capable of mispricing.** The same logic also explains why existing investment strategies may work better in some environments than others. When optimism is widespread and short selling is difficult, overpriced securities can remain overpriced because pessimistic investors cannot fully express their views.

Mispricing therefore becomes asymmetric. Optimistic investors can buy as much as their capital allows. Pessimistic investors may simply stay out. That makes high-sentiment periods particularly fertile for overvaluation, especially on the short side of long-short strategies. The broader lesson is that behavioral signals become much more powerful when they are combined with market structure. Sentiment alone is noisy. Valuation characteristics alone may be weak. But when I understand who is likely to be biased, which assets are most exposed to that bias, and what prevents other investors from correcting the price, the mechanism becomes much clearer.

The best behavioral investment ideas may therefore come not from asking whether investors are irrational. They come from asking where irrationality has the greatest room to survive.
