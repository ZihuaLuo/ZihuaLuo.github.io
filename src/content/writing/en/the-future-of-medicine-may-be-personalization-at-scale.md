> The real promise of big data in medicine is not finding one treatment that works for everyone, but making individualized treatment scalable enough to work for millions.

Cancer is difficult partly because the word itself hides enormous variation. Two patients may receive the same diagnosis while the biological mechanisms driving their diseases are different. Even within one patient, the disease can change over time. A treatment that works initially may become less effective as the underlying cells evolve. This creates a fundamental problem for traditional medicine. Medicine becomes easier to scale when many patients can receive essentially the same treatment. An antibiotic works because physicians can identify a common biological target and use a standardized drug against it.

Cancer is often much less uniform. The ideal solution would therefore be intensely personalized: understand the specific abnormalities affecting one patient, choose a treatment for those abnormalities, monitor how the disease changes, and adjust the treatment again. The problem is that true personalization is expensive. If every patient required a separate research team to discover a completely new treatment, individualized medicine would remain available only in exceptional cases. The scientific idea might work, but the economics would not.

This is where I find the role of big data more interesting. The goal is not necessarily to invent a new drug for every person. It is to convert millions of individual differences into a sufficiently large but still manageable set of recurring biological patterns. If enough genomic, clinical, and treatment data can be collected, machine learning can help identify which combinations of abnormalities tend to produce particular diseases and which treatments work best for each pattern. **Then personalization becomes a matching problem.**

Instead of asking, “How do we create a unique treatment for this patient?” the question becomes, “Which known biological pattern does this patient most closely resemble, and which treatment has worked best for that pattern?” That difference changes the economics completely. A problem that is impossibly large when handled one patient at a time can become manageable when knowledge is accumulated across millions of patients and reused. This is a broader lesson about what data does well. Big data is most powerful when variation is too complex for humans to process case by case but structured enough that patterns repeat across a large population.

The same principle appears outside medicine. A good recommendation system does not create an entirely new theory of taste for every user. It learns from millions of previous behaviors and finds people or situations that resemble one another. A credit model does not need to understand every borrower from first principles. It looks for patterns that have appeared before. Medicine is obviously more difficult because the consequences are much higher and biological systems are far more complex. But the underlying logic is similar. The future of personalized medicine may therefore depend on an apparent contradiction: The more individual the treatment becomes, the more collective the underlying data must be. **Personalization does not eliminate scale.** **It requires scale to become affordable.**
