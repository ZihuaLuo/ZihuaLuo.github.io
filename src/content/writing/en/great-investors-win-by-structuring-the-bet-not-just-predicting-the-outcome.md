> A strong investment thesis matters, but the structure of the payoff, the cost of financing, and the ability to survive long enough for the thesis to work can matter even more.

It is easy to describe great investors by the calls they got right. John Paulson predicted the collapse of subprime mortgages. George Soros recognized that the British pound was unsustainably overvalued. Warren Buffett repeatedly identified exceptional companies. But this lecture made me think that prediction alone is not what made these investments extraordinary. The more important skill was structuring the bet so that being right mattered much more than being wrong.

Paulson's subprime trade is a clear example. His thesis was that the market dramatically underestimated the probability of mortgage defaults. But simply believing that housing was in a bubble would not have been enough. Housing could remain expensive for years, and shorting the wrong instrument could destroy a fund before the correction arrived. What made the trade powerful was its asymmetry. The ongoing cost of betting against subprime securities was relatively limited, while a severe deterioration in the underlying mortgages could produce an enormous payoff. Because the downside of waiting was manageable relative to the potential upside, Paulson could build a much larger position than he could have in a conventional short.

Soros approached the pound with similar logic. The pound was being maintained within the European Exchange Rate Mechanism even though Britain's economic conditions made that exchange rate increasingly difficult to defend. If the peg survived, the potential adverse move appeared relatively constrained. If Britain abandoned it, however, the currency could fall much further. Again, the important insight was not simply “the pound is overvalued.” It was that the payoff distribution was unusually favorable. This changes how I think about conviction. Large positions should not come merely from being extremely confident. Confidence can be wrong. A large position becomes more defensible when the structure itself limits the consequences of being wrong while preserving substantial upside if the thesis is correct.

Buffett illustrates a different version of the same principle. His advantage was not only choosing stocks with attractive fundamentals. Much of his long-term success can be understood as owning high-quality companies — businesses with strong profitability, stable earnings, lower risk, and durable economics — while financing those assets with unusually cheap and persistent capital from insurance float. That combination matters. A good asset financed badly can still become a bad investment. Expensive or unstable leverage can force an investor to sell at exactly the wrong moment. Buffett's financing, in contrast, has historically been unusually patient. That allows him to hold positions through periods when the market disagrees with him.

His performance during the late 1990s is a useful reminder. Berkshire dramatically underperformed the broader market while technology stocks surged. A conventional fund experiencing similar relative losses could have faced withdrawals and forced selling. Buffett could survive. That survival itself was part of the strategy. This connects the three investors more than their individual investment styles initially suggest. Paulson structured a trade where the potential payoff dwarfed the carrying cost. Soros concentrated capital when the downside appeared bounded relative to the upside. Buffett combined high-quality assets with financing that reduced the probability of being forced out during temporary underperformance.

None of them depended only on knowing what would happen next. They paid close attention to what would happen if they were wrong, how long they could remain wrong, and whether they would still be around when the thesis finally proved correct. The broader lesson is that investing is not only about expected return. It is about the shape of the entire payoff. Before asking how much I can make if I am right, I should understand how much I can lose if I am wrong, what it costs to wait, what could force me to exit early, and whether the opportunity is attractive enough to justify meaningful size.

The best investment may not be the one with the most convincing forecast. It may be the one in which I do not need to be right very often for being right to matter enormously.
