> Good management turns abstract principles into specific actions that ordinary people can repeat, measure, and improve.

Many ideas sound correct but are difficult to use.

- “Serve customers better.”
- “Improve product quality.”
- “Build a respected company.”
- “Encourage innovation.”

Almost nobody disagrees with these statements. That is precisely the problem. If everyone can agree with a goal without changing what they do tomorrow morning, the goal has almost no operational value. This is the distinction between understanding a principle and building a system around it. The contrast between *The Art of War* and Clausewitz’s *On War* offers an interesting analogy. Sun Tzu provides remarkably broad strategic principles, many of which remain relevant thousands of years later. But principles alone do not necessarily tell an inexperienced commander exactly what to do.

Execution still depends heavily on judgment. A more operational framework tries to reduce that dependence. It asks whether knowledge can be translated into procedures that someone else can learn, repeat, and eventually improve. This distinction matters even more inside organizations. A small company can depend on a few exceptional people. The founder knows the customers, makes the important decisions, solves conflicts, and personally maintains the standard of the product. But this stops working as the organization grows. Human attention does not scale at the same rate as headcount.

A manager cannot personally explain every decision to hundreds or thousands of employees. If quality depends entirely on one leader’s judgment, then quality deteriorates as soon as that leader is absent. The solution is not necessarily to find more exceptional managers. It is to convert good judgment into repeatable mechanisms. Suppose a software company says that product quality matters. That statement changes almost nothing. A more useful goal might be to increase automated test coverage from 5% to 50%.

Now the organization can design concrete rules around it: code without adequate testing cannot be merged, teams track coverage regularly, and incentives reward improvements. “Improve quality” has become observable behavior. That is the difference between management by intention and management by system. The same principle applies to personal work. If I say I want to “become a better writer,” the goal is too vague to guide behavior. If I decide to publish one carefully revised essay each week, collect feedback, record recurring weaknesses, and review those notes before the next essay, improvement becomes much more likely.

**The vision still matters.** **But vision must eventually descend into something I can do.** This also explains why documentation and process are so important. A breakthrough that exists only inside one person’s head is fragile. Once that person leaves, the organization may have to rediscover the solution. A system allows knowledge to accumulate. The broader lesson is that great ideas become valuable only when they survive contact with ordinary execution. Whenever I hear an impressive principle, I should therefore ask: What behavior would look different tomorrow if we genuinely believed this? If there is no clear answer, the principle may be inspiring. But it is not yet management.
