> Intelligence helps us find ways around constraints. Judgment tells us which opportunities are not worth exploiting in the first place.

Technology gives people leverage. A person clicking manually can never compete with a program making thousands of requests automatically. In many competitive environments, this is exactly why technology matters. Companies that learn to use software, automation, and artificial intelligence effectively will outperform those that insist on doing everything manually. But greater capability creates a second question: Just because I can do something more efficiently, should I do it at all? The Alibaba mooncake incident illustrates the distinction.

Several engineers wrote programs that allowed them to purchase a disproportionate number of mooncakes offered internally to employees. From a purely technical perspective, the solution was clever. They understood the system better than most participants and used that knowledge to gain an advantage. But the system was never intended to be a programming competition. The mooncakes were effectively an employee benefit distributed through a limited opportunity. Automating the process did not create additional value. It simply transferred opportunities from colleagues who were playing under the intended rules to those who understood how to exploit the mechanism.

This is an important distinction between competition and exploitation. Using superior technology to create a better product is competition. Using superior technology to capture a larger share of a benefit that was intended to be distributed fairly is something different. The more interesting mistake, however, is not ethical but economic. The potential gain was tiny. The downside was enormous. Losing a valuable job, damaging a professional reputation, or reducing future career opportunities in exchange for a few boxes of mooncakes is an extraordinarily poor trade.

Yet intelligent people make decisions like this surprisingly often. An engineer sells access to something that should not be sold. An employee uses an internal advantage to make a few hundred dollars. Someone spends hours exploiting a promotional system while neglecting work that could materially advance a career. The behavior may demonstrate ingenuity. It also demonstrates an inability to distinguish what is valuable from what is merely available. This is what separates cleverness from judgment. **Cleverness asks:** How can I get this?

**Judgment asks:** Why do I want this, what am I risking, and is it worth my attention? That second question becomes increasingly important as technology makes exploitation easier. Automation allows one person to do at enormous scale what previously required substantial effort. AI will extend that leverage further. But technology amplifies intentions; it does not improve them automatically. **A person with poor judgment and more powerful tools may simply make larger mistakes faster.** There is also a career lesson here.

Time spent capturing trivial advantages has an opportunity cost. If meaningful work is effectively unlimited, spending several hours designing a system to obtain a small personal benefit means choosing not to spend those hours solving a difficult problem, learning something valuable, or creating something that compounds. The immediate reward is visible. The lost opportunity is not. That is why small advantages can be unexpectedly expensive. The broader lesson is not that we should avoid using technology aggressively. In real competition, technological leverage is often essential. But before optimizing any opportunity, I should first decide whether the opportunity deserves to be optimized. A strong career is rarely destroyed because someone lacked enough small advantages. It is much more easily damaged by repeatedly being smart about things that never mattered.
