> Limited attention causes us to overweight information that is visible, recent, or easy to process while neglecting information that may matter far more.

We often assume that important information will naturally receive more attention. In reality, the direction of causality can run the other way: something can appear important simply because it has captured our attention.

The Mars example makes this surprisingly clear. When NASA's Pathfinder mission landed on Mars, media coverage repeatedly exposed people to the word “Mars.” Nothing about the chocolate itself had changed, yet sales increased. The product became easier to recall, and that small increase in mental availability was enough to influence purchasing behavior.

The same mechanism affects judgments that seem much more serious.

When the media disproportionately reports a particular type of accident, crime, or rare event, we begin to estimate its probability based on how easily examples come to mind rather than how frequently the event actually occurs. A vivid event can therefore feel common, while an ordinary event that happens every day can become almost invisible.

This is one consequence of limited attention. We cannot process every piece of information around us, so the brain uses accessibility as a shortcut. Recent, emotional, familiar, and simple information receives more weight because it is easier to retrieve.

That shortcut is efficient, but it creates an important distinction:

**Visibility and importance are not the same thing.**

Financial markets provide some of the clearest examples. During the internet bubble, companies could add “.com” to their names and experience large increases in valuation even when the underlying business barely changed. Similar effects appeared with companies attaching themselves to popular themes such as blockchain. Investors were not necessarily conducting a new valuation of future cash flows. Their attention had simply been redirected toward a fashionable signal.

This initially looks absurd, but a subtler version of the same mistake probably happens constantly.

Investors naturally notice information that is easy to observe: price movements, headlines, earnings surprises, popular industries, or a company's name. Much more important information can be harder to process. R&D efficiency is a good example. Knowing how much a company spends on research is easy. Determining how effectively that spending produces valuable patents is much harder.

Because the information requires more work, fewer investors incorporate it into prices.

That creates an interesting inversion. The information receiving the least attention may sometimes contain the greatest opportunity precisely because it has not yet been fully reflected in the market.

This changes how I think about both investing and everyday judgment. When something feels unusually important, I should ask whether it is actually important or simply unusually salient. And when something appears boring, complicated, or difficult to measure, I should not assume that it is irrelevant.

Often the most valuable information is not hidden.

It is visible to everyone, but inconvenient enough that few people are willing to pay attention to it.
