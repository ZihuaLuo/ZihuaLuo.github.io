> A model becomes dangerous not when it simplifies reality, but when we forget what it has simplified away.

The first lecture of Behavioral Economics appears to be an introduction to the debate between rational and behavioral economics. But the more important question behind that debate is how we should use models. Economics needs simplification. The problem begins only when a useful simplification is mistaken for reality itself.

Traditional economics often starts with a rational individual: someone who understands incentives, processes information correctly, and chooses what maximizes personal utility. This assumption creates elegant models and gives us a clean benchmark for thinking about decisions. But real outcomes depend not only on what is rational in theory, but also on how actual people behave.

The ultimatum game makes this difference obvious. If one person divides $100 and the other can only accept or reject the offer, strict rationality suggests that even a $1 offer should be accepted. One dollar is still better than nothing. Following that logic, the proposer should keep almost everything.

In reality, people often reject extremely unequal offers. They are willing to sacrifice money to punish something they consider unfair. Fairness, pride, anger, and social expectations enter a decision that looks much simpler in the model.

The lesson is not simply that people are irrational. The deeper problem is that my own rationality does not guarantee a good outcome if my assumptions about everyone else are wrong.

The guessing game illustrates this even more clearly. When everyone chooses a number between 0 and 100 and tries to be closest to two-thirds of the average, perfect rational reasoning eventually pushes the answer toward zero. Yet choosing zero does not necessarily win. Winning requires estimating how deeply everyone else will reason. The theoretically correct answer and the practically successful answer can be different.

This matters in markets, negotiations, organizations, and investing because decisions rarely happen in isolation. I am responding to other people, while they are responding to what they believe others will do. Understanding the benchmark is useful, but understanding deviations from the benchmark can be just as important.

The comparison with physics is especially useful. Ignoring air resistance does not make a basic physics model wrong. It allows us to isolate the underlying mechanism. But the same assumption becomes inadequate when we try to explain how an airplane flies.

Behavioral economics plays a similar role. It does not need to replace rational economics. It asks when the forces excluded from the simpler model become important enough to change the outcome.

The broader lesson is therefore not to distrust models, but to understand their boundaries. A good model tells us what should happen under a specific set of assumptions. Good judgment begins with recognizing when those assumptions no longer describe the world in front of us.
